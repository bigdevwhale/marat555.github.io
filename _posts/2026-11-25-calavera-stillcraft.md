Mexico's Day of the Dead treats death as a guest at the party, not a monster under the bed. Calavera brings that spirit to ageing and mortality, with colour and a lot of jokes.

Most of us were taught to look away from death, so it waits in the corner and grows. In Mexico, on the first nights of November, people do the opposite: they build altars, dress up as skeletons and write funny poems about their own funerals. Calavera brings that spirit to a phone: dress your skeleton, build an altar for the ones you miss, draw a card for each fear, write a funny verse about your own send-off. A few minutes a day, with colour and jokes, to make the end a little less frightening and the years a little brighter. Free, no ads, no account, no network.

## What's inside

### Your calaca

The home screen is you, without the skin: a skeleton that sways under a night sky and dances when tapped. Dress it from the wardrobe: flower crowns, sombreros, Catrina's hat, a charro suit, a fiesta dress, a guitar, maracas, five face paints. Clothes are bought with marigold petals, earned by the practices.

### Privileges of years

Some things can't be bought. Glasses, a cane, a rebozo shawl and a gold tooth come with age: Catrina brings the next one on each birthday. You arrive with one for each decade past thirty. Ageing is an upgrade, not a loss.

{% include figure.html path="blog/calavera-post/shot-1.jpg" caption="The ofrenda. The altar under an arch of marigolds, with portraits and candles." %}

### Catrina's visit

Every day a word from La Catrina ("A wrinkle is where a smile has been") and a small errand: look at the sky for a minute, call a grandparent, eat something slowly. One quiet notice a day, at the hour you choose.

### The ofrenda

A drawn altar under an arch of marigolds. Put up the people and pets you miss, with a photo or a painted card, set out what they loved (coffee, pan de muerto, music, a cigar), light a candle once a day and keep their memories. From the 31st of October to the 2nd of November the altar glows.

{% include figure.html path="blog/calavera-post/shot-2.jpg" caption="Nana Rosa. Her page: a lit candle, offerings and memories." %}

### Lotería of fears

Twelve cards, one a day: El Olvido (being forgotten), La Nada (nothingness), El Reloj (running out of time), El Espejo (the face in the mirror). Turn the card over for Catrina's take on that fear and a two-minute exercise. Collect the deck.

### Calaveritas

Write a calaverita literaria, the traditional funny verse in which Death comes for someone, about yourself. Start from Catrina's examples, then share it as a card with papel picado and your calaca.

{% include figure.html path="blog/calavera-post/shot-3.jpg" caption="The practices. Everything there is to do, on one list." %}

### Papel picado

Fold the tissue in half or in four, cut it with your finger, unfold it. It hangs on the garland over your calaca for a week, fading and drooping, then the wind takes it. Impermanence you can hold.

### Marigold road

Irvin Yalom's "rippling": each marigold is someone or something that will carry a trace of you. The road grows.

{% include figure.html path="blog/calavera-post/shot-4.jpg" caption="La Soledad. A lotería card turned over: Catrina's reflection and an exercise." %}

### Fear thermometer

Eight statements once a month; watch the temperature fall.

### My last fiesta

Plan the send-off like a party: music, food, who speaks, how to be remembered, wishes about care, where the papers are. Share it as text when you're ready.

{% include figure.html path="blog/calavera-post/shot-5.jpg" caption="Calaveritas. Funny verses about your own death." %}

### Pan de muerto

Thirty questions for the table, to talk about life and death with family. Pass the phone round.

### Fiesta of the elders

People who did their best work late, and Becca Levy's finding that those who think well of ageing live about 7.5 years longer.

## The practices

| Practice | What you do | How often |
|---|---|---|
| **Catrina's visit** | Read her word, do a small errand | Every day |
| **The ofrenda** | Light a candle, add an offering or a memory | A candle a day |
| **Lotería of fears** | Turn over a card, do a two-minute exercise | A card a day, twelve in all |
| **Calaveritas** | Write a funny verse about yourself | Whenever the muse comes |
| **Papel picado** | Fold, cut, unfold, hang it up | Each flag lasts a week |
| **Fear thermometer** | Eight statements | Once a month |
| **Pan de muerto** | Questions for the family table | At a family meal |

## Good to know

**Isn't an app about death a bit grim?**

That's the point of the Day of the Dead: death as a guest at the party, not a monster under the bed. Calavera is colourful and full of jokes, and it treats growing old as an upgrade.

**What if the dark thoughts are about now?**

Then please talk to someone. If something you write sounds that way, Calavera gently says so and links to findahelpline.com, where you can find a free, confidential helpline in your country. The link is always in the settings too.

**Where do the photos for the altar go?**

They are copied into the app's own storage and never leave the phone. You can also use a painted card instead of a photo.

**Does it need the internet?**

No. It doesn't even ask for the permission. No account, no analytics, no ads.

**What if I change phones?**

Settings → Backup saves the ofrenda and its photos, verses, flags, readings, wardrobe and plan into one zip. Restore it on the new phone.
