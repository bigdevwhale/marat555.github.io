I tried a dozen practice apps, and they all failed the same way: one reminder, one practice, one streak. My day doesn't work like that. In the morning I want three breaths and a question, at noon I want to stop for a minute, and at night I want to look back at the day. What I wanted was a shape for the whole day, and monasteries worked one out fifteen hundred years ago.

A medieval book of hours divided the day into hours of prayer, counted by the sun and rung by a bell. Horae keeps that frame and leaves the rest to you: how many hours there are, when they ring, and what each one holds. Everything you keep is written into an illuminated manuscript. Free, no ads, no account, no internet.

## What's inside

### Unequal hours

The old reckoning didn't use sixty-minute hours. The light from sunrise to sunset was twelve hours, and so was the dark. A summer day hour ran past eighty minutes, a winter one under fifty. Horae works this out on the phone from where you are, with no network. The first screen is a dial: the day's twelve hours on top, the night's twelve below, your hours on the rim, and the sun where it stands right now.

{% include figure.html path="blog/horae-post/shot-1.jpg" caption="The dial of unequal hours: in Moscow in late October, a day hour lasts 49 minutes." %}

### Your rule

You can have one hour or seven, or more. Each one is anchored:
- **by the sun:** sunrise, the third hour, noon, sunset, an hour and a half after it;
- **by the clock:** at 8:00;
- **by hand:** it doesn't ring and you open it when you like.

Each hour has its own weekdays and its own bell: a great bell, a small bell, a handbell, a singing bowl or a zen wooden board that speeds up as it's struck. The bells are synthesised from real bell partials. You can also start from a ready rule (Benedictine, Stoic, zen, Hesychast, a day of metta, or five minutes with no religion) and rework it.

### Thirteen practices

An hour is a list of practices in the order you choose, each with its own settings:
- **breathing:** box, 4-7-8, even, long exhale, alternate nostril, or your own pattern;
- **meditation:** on the breath, a body scan, noting or open awareness, with interval bells;
- **metta:** your own phrases, travelling round the circle of people;
- **beads:** lestovka, mala, tasbih or rosary. Any count, moved by a tap or the volume keys, so you needn't look at the screen;
- **reading:** psalms, Ecclesiastes, the desert fathers, Thomas à Kempis, the Stoics, the Tao, the Dhammapada, zen, the Sufis, or texts of your own, with lectio divina;
- **a question**, **thanks**, **a review of the day** (Ignatius, Seneca, Franklin or your own steps), **bows**, **slow walking**, **a candle**, **silence** that counts only while the phone lies face down, and **steps of your own**.

Every hour can also carry a one-minute short rule for a busy day or the road.

### The manuscript

A kept hour goes onto the day's page in red rubrics and brown ink: the answer, the reading, the word you chose in red, the thanks. Each practice leaves its mark in the margin: a wavy line for breathing, a row of dots for beads, a candle for gazing. A vine grows along the edge. The opening initial starts in red ink, turns lapis, then burnished gold as more of the day's hours are kept. A missed hour stays an empty space, with no reproach. Creatures come to the margins: a snail, a hare scribe, a cat, and on days you kept noon, the noonday demon, the monks' name for boredom.

Yesterday's page can't be rewritten. You can only gloss it in the margin, a note from the future in red.

### The calendar and the great silence

Each month opens with its own leaf: the labour of the month, as in the old calendars, and the zodiac arch with the sun where it really stands. Your own red-letter days are written in red. Seasons of your own, forty days of something, colour the pages violet, green, white, red or rose. After the last hour of the day, the book closes until the first bell tomorrow. A long press breaks the silence, and the page remembers. On 31 December the year is bound in leather and goes on the shelf.

## Good to know

**Is it religious?**

It borrows a monastery's way of dividing the day, not its faith. The Benedictine and Hesychast rules sit next to the Stoic, the zen and the five-minute one, and any hour can be rebuilt from scratch. The readings are my own renderings from public-domain originals.

**The bell is silent.**

On silent the bell only vibrates, unless you turn on "Ring even on silent". On Xiaomi, Huawei and similar phones, allow autostart too, or the system may cancel the alarms.
