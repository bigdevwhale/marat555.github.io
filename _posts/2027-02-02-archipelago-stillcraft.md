Making friends as an adult turned out to be less about meeting people and more about seeing them again. Jeffrey Hall counted it in 2018: about 50 hours together turn an acquaintance into a casual friend, about 90 into a friend, over 200 into a close friend. I met plenty of nice people at quizzes and climbing gyms and saw most of them exactly once. So I built a chart to keep them on.

An old sea chart of your people. You're the island in the middle; everyone you meet is an island around you, and the hours you spend together build the bridge between you. Small sorties of courage help you meet people, a paper boat reminds you of the second time, and fog rolls in when you stop. Free, no ads, no account, no internet.

## What's inside

### Everyone is an island

No photo, no profile. A name, one detail that stuck ("keeps bees on the roof") and up to four things they love, drawn as little engraved landmarks on the shore: mountains, a coffee house, a cat, a die.

### Sorties of courage

A ladder of six rungs in ordinary life: a nod, a question, a name, praise, a contact, an invitation. Three tries a rung, then the open sea, with bolder things like introducing two friends or suggesting a regular evening. The try counts, not the answer: every "no" brings a shell of courage, and every tenth shell is a nautilus.

### The second time

The hardest part of a new friendship isn't meeting — it's the second step. A new island gets a paper boat and a week. On its card are ready messages to copy into any messenger; send one and the island drops anchor. Miss the week and it drifts, but it's never too late.

{% include figure.html path="blog/archipelago-post/shot-1.jpg" caption="Marta, from the quiz. Five days left for the paper boat." %}

### Hours build bridges

After you meet, log roughly how long you were together: half an hour, two hours, all day. The bridge grows on Hall's scale — rope, planks at 50 hours, stone arches at 90, a lantern arch at 200 — and the island drifts closer to yours. Every year you've known someone plants a pennant on the bridge.

{% include figure.html path="blog/archipelago-post/shot-2.jpg" caption="Dima: 78 hours, 12 more to a stone bridge." %}

### Creaking and fog

A month without meeting and the bridge sags and creaks. Half a year without a word and the island is lost in the fog. It doesn't disappear: one message brings it back out, and then it's time to meet.

### Ferries and the lighthouse

Introduce two of your people and a ferry runs between their islands, so the chart becomes a web instead of a star. Have people over and light the lighthouse: everyone who came gets the hours, and those who hadn't met get a ferry too.

### Sailing notes and harbours

A few lines to remember for next time: the exam on Friday, the dog's name. Harbours are the places you keep going back to — a class, a choir, the yard — because friendships grow where the same faces keep turning up.

{% include figure.html path="blog/archipelago-post/shot-3.jpg" caption="Today's sortie, the ladder and the shells." %}

### Kept for years

The Atlas holds thirteen long expeditions, your harbours and a summary of every year: new islands, hours together, bridges raised, who you spent the most time with. The ship's log keeps everything, and the whole chart saves to one file for your next phone.

## How a bridge grows

| Bridge | Hours together | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| **Rope** | 0–50 | An acquaintance |
| **Planks** | 50 | A casual friend |
| **Stone arches** | 90 | A friend |
| **Lantern arch** | 200 | A close friend |

## Good to know

**Does it find people for me?**

No — it has no network at all. It sends you to the people already in your life and helps you come back to them.

**Do I have to log every meeting exactly?**

Roughly is enough. The bridges are about the slow sum of years, not minutes.

**What if I'm an introvert?**

Skip sorties as often as you like and put your time into the bridges you already have; those hours count the same.
