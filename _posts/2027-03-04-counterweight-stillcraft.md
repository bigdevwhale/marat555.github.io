At three in the morning a worry feels like a fact. "They'll lay me off." "The money won't last till payday." "That mole has changed colour." In daylight I know that most of these never happen. But at night there is nothing on the other side of the scale, and the worry wins without a fight.

So I built a scale. You put the worry on the left pan of an old brass balance, as a stone. On the right pan the app lays weights one by one, each with a single argument on it, until the beam tips. There are 1,209 concrete worries in 24 spheres, from money and work to death and the news, and 15,900 weights. Free, no ads, no account, no internet.

## What's inside

### The stone

You type the worry in your own words or pick it from the shelves. Search knows how people really put things, so "afraid I'll be laid off" finds "They'll make me redundant". Then you say how heavy it is, from a grain of sand to a whole rock, and the stone drops into the pan with a thud.

### Eight metals

The weights aren't all the same, because arguments aren't. Each metal rings its own note on the pan:

- **Cast iron:** a fact, with its source.
- **Brass:** "and then what?", the worst case walked through step by step until it reaches solid ground.
- **Copper:** one small thing within your control today.
- **Silver:** the other side of the same stone.
- **Glass:** a question worth asking yourself.
- **Bronze:** a voice, from Seneca to the psychologists.
- **A feather:** a little kind humour. Sometimes the feather is what tips it.

This isn't "think positive". Fears that can truly come true, like a diagnosis or a parent's death, get **steel**: honest weights that don't promise it'll all be fine. A weight that isn't for you can be set aside, and it never comes back.

### Checking the scales

When the beam tips, you say when it will be clear whether the worry came true: tomorrow, in a week, in a month. On that morning the app asks, "So, did it happen?" Over time your own record becomes a weight too, sealed in red wax: *"You checked 23 worries. 17 didn't happen. Of those that did, you got through 6 of 6."* Nobody else's numbers weigh as much as your own, so next time this one goes on the pan early.

{% include figure.html path="blog/counterweight-post/shot-2.jpg" caption="The ledger: what came true, and what didn't." %}

### Polished to a pebble

Stones lie on wooden shelves by sphere. Each time the same worry is outweighed, its stone gets a little smoother, until it's a pebble you can throw into the river. There's a splash, and one stone fewer.

{% include figure.html path="blog/counterweight-post/shot-3.jpg" caption="The shelves, and the river at the bottom." %}

### The well

"And then what?" all the way down. The well takes the worst case one step at a time: you're laid off, then there's notice and severance, then CVs and interviews, then a new job, maybe not the first one. At the bottom is what holds you up, written by you: a sister who always picks up, the fact that you know how to start again, friends in another city.

{% include figure.html path="blog/counterweight-post/shot-4.jpg" caption="At the bottom of the well." %}

### The case, the hour for worries, the night

- **The case:** a velvet case for the weights that hit home. You can engrave your own too, and your own arguments weigh more than anyone else's.
- **The hour for worries:** put a stone on the shelf until evening and don't carry it all day.
- **At night:** between midnight and five the chamber goes dark and has softer weights of its own, because at 3 a.m. the mind exaggerates.
- **A safety screen:** if the words are about self-harm, the scale doesn't open at all. A screen with numbers to call opens instead.

## Good to know

**Does it replace therapy?**

No. It's a scale, not a doctor. If it has been hard for a long time, it's worth going to one.

**What leaves the phone?**

Nothing. The app has no internet permission at all. A weight leaves only when you share it yourself.

## Get it
