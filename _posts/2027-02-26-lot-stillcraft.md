Life simulators like BitLife or 1 in 8 billion make you one of the people alive today. That's a strange sample. About 117 billion people have ever been born, and most of them lived and died long before anyone kept a census. If you were really drawn at random from everyone who ever lived, you'd most likely be a peasant somewhere before 1800, and you'd have nearly an even chance of not reaching five.

Lot draws from all of them. It's a lottery bag of every human life, a parish register to read yours in, and a chest for what each life leaves behind. Free, no ads, no account, no internet.

## What's inside

### A fair draw

The sack follows the Population Reference Bureau's estimate of how many people have ever been born:
- **Stone Age**, before 8000 BC: about 9 billion;
- **the ancient world**: 37 billion;
- **the two middle ages**, up to 1650: 39 billion;
- **the early modern centuries**, up to 1850: 9 billion;
- **the age of machines**, up to 1950: 8 billion;
- **our time**: 15 billion.

Within an era the draw is weighted by region and by family: peasants, slaves, serfs, craftsmen, merchants, temple scribes, a noble house. You shake the sack and a wooden tag rises out of it with the year, the place, the family and boy or girl burnt into it. Beside it are the odds: what share of everyone who ever lived was born like you, how many children there live to five, and how many people around you can read.

### The parish register

The life is written into a register in the registrar's hand, a few years per page: childhood, work, a match, hard times, old age, death. There are about two hundred everyday lines across six ages, in English and Russian. Real history reaches the life from the side, as about seventy actual events: the Black Death takes one neighbour in three, the Mongol hosts arrive, the first satellite crawls across the night sky. Now and then you pass someone later written about. Socrates argues with you in the square for half an hour, and Ibn Battuta writes down how you bake bread.

### Shut doors

At each fork every door is shown, and some are shut. They're struck through, with the reason in red: *Girls are not taught their letters. You are not free to leave. You are given no choice: the elders have already agreed.* I didn't want the app to lecture about inequality. It's enough to see which doors were never open.

If you'd rather not choose at all, be a witness: fate makes every choice and you only read.

### The chest and the great-grandchildren

Each life leaves one thing: a bone needle, a clay tablet recording a debt, a greatcoat button, a push-button phone. After a life with children you can draw a great-grandchild's lot instead of a new one. They're born 70 to 94 years later in the same place, with the surname, the family's means and the same thing. Over a few lives the thing gathers its holders, one per generation.

### A question for you

There are no points and no winning. A life ends with an epitaph and one quiet question about you today. *This person never saw the sea. When was the sea last in front of your eyes?* Or: *The elders decided everything for her. What in your last year was your own choice?* The answers go into a notebook.

### The atlas and the census

Your birthplaces are pinned on an old map, with a slider through time. Next to it, your lots stand beside the real spread of everyone ever born, so after a few dozen lives you can see the odds for yourself. Every life is replayed from a seed and your choices, so any book in the archive can be read again in full.

## Good to know

**Why am I almost always a peasant?**

Because most people who ever lived were. More than 80 of the 117 billion were born before 1800, and nearly all of them worked the land.

**Is it accurate?**

The numbers are rounded estimates, and the lines are written to be true to their age rather than to one particular village. It's a way to feel the odds, not a history textbook.
