I don't believe in tarot. But I like the pictures on the 1909 Rider–Waite deck, and I like a particular kind of aunt: the one who reads your cards at the kitchen table and somehow finds good news in all of them.

There's also a bit of real psychology hiding here. Richard Wiseman, who spent years studying people who call themselves lucky, found that they mostly notice more: the chance, the kind word, the small good thing. So I made a fortune-teller whose readings are always lucky, and whose every card comes with something small and concrete to notice today. Silver Lining is free, with no ads, no account and no internet.

## What's inside

### The table

A checked oilcloth, a candle in a brass saucer and a glass of tea with lemon. To shuffle, shake the phone or rub the deck with a finger; then cut it towards yourself. The cards fly out face down, and you turn each one over yourself. Above the table, the Auntie comments in her own hand. If her handwriting is hard to read, she'll switch to print.

### No bad cards

All 78 cards have two readings: upright, and upside down, which always means the worst is already behind you. The frightening ones get the most care:

- **Death** is the card of moving out of the old. Look at the edge of the picture: the sun is rising between two towers, and a child is holding out flowers.
- **The Tower** is finally knocking down what you never got round to knocking down.
- **The Ten of Swords**: ten was overdoing it, one would have done. The worst has already happened, and there's a gold dawn on the horizon.

{% include figure.html path="blog/silverlining-post/shot-1.jpg" caption="The lucky cross: five cards for something big." %}

### An omen for today

Every card brings an omen: a small thing that really can happen today. Someone will hold a door for you. An old friend will write first. The lights will be green all the way. There are 936 of them, twelve to a card, and none of them is about money or fate.

### “Well, did it come true?”

In the evening the Auntie asks. If it came true, you write one line about how, and it goes into the box. If it didn't, she isn't disappointed: “then it's saving up, tomorrow twice over.” One quiet notification a day, at the hour you choose.

{% include figure.html path="blog/silverlining-post/shot-2.jpg" caption="The evening question." %}

### A question

Ask anything, and the answer is always “yes, and here's how”, followed by one small step the card suggests for today.

{% include figure.html path="blog/silverlining-post/shot-3.jpg" caption="Even the Tower says yes." %}

### The box and the deck

Everything that came true stays in the box with your lines. On a hard day, ask her to read you what already happened, and she'll take out three of them. The deck remembers too: cards that come true often wear soft corners, a crease, a ring from a glass of tea. The one that comes true most becomes your card and falls a little more often.

### More spreads

For the road, for a meeting, for the week (an omen for each day), and a reading for a friend sent as a postcard. At Christmastide, at Midsummer and on your birthday there are spreads of their own.

## Good to know

**Is this real fortune-telling?**

No, and the Auntie doesn't pretend it is. The cards give your eye a small task for the day.

**What leaves the phone?**

Nothing. The app has no internet permission at all.

## Get it
