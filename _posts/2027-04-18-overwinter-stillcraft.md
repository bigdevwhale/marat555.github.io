I've started a wellbeing diary at least four times. Each one went the same way: "rate your mood from 1 to 10" every evening, a week of dutiful numbers, then nothing. The questions never changed, and nothing ever came back from them.

Overwinter turns the same diary into a wintering. You are alone at a polar station, and the station is your body. Three times a day comes a synoptic term: ten seconds to read the instruments and send a report to the mainland. It's free, with no ads and no internet access at all.

## What's inside

### Real scales, ten seconds

The instruments are the ones meteorologists actually use:

- **Wind is energy**, on the Beaufort scale: 0 is "calm, flat out", 12 is "hurricane, I could move mountains".
- **Cloud is mood**, in eighths of the sky: 0 is clear and light, 8 is grey with no gaps.
- **Visibility** is how clear the head is: fog, haze, good, excellent.
- **Precipitation is pain.** Tap the body where it hurts: once for drizzle, twice for rain, three times for a thunderstorm.

The morning term also asks how long you slept. A "weather box" holds the things you want to watch, such as coffee, alcohol, a workout, a pill or a screen late at night, and you can add your own.

{% include figure.html path="blog/overwinter-post/shot-1.jpg" caption="An evening term." %}

### The telegram

The report goes out the way real stations send theirs: five-figure groups on a paper tape, with the telegraph key tapping out the start in Morse. It's a small ritual, and it makes the ten seconds feel like a job worth doing.

{% include figure.html path="blog/overwinter-post/shot-2.jpg" caption="Off to the mainland." %}

### Cyclones with names

Two bad days in a row make a cyclone, and it gets a name, the way weather services name storms. A good spell becomes a high. The barogram draws thirty days in one pencil line, with the pressure dashed underneath. When a bad spell drags on, it helps to look back and see the last one end.

{% include figure.html path="blog/overwinter-post/shot-3.jpg" caption="Thirty days and Cyclone Boris." %}

### The observer's manual

The station compares days with each instrument and without it and writes down what repeats: "The day after coffee, you have less energy." "When the pressure falls 4 hPa in a day, something hurts more often." You keep what's true and strike out what's chance. If the phone has a barometer, the station records the pressure every hour and warns you the next time it starts to fall.

{% include figure.html path="blog/overwinter-post/shot-4.jpg" caption="What repeats." %}

### A station that grows

The station is rewarded for observing, not for feeling well, because a migraine shouldn't cost you anything. With every term a new building appears: a weather screen, an anemometer, a flag mast, a wind turbine, Purga the dog, a banya, and eventually an icebreaker at the pier. Rare sights turn up for a reason: the aurora comes the first fair term after a cyclone has passed, and reindeer tracks wait by the door when you come back after a break. Missed days aren't punished. The station just gets snowed in, and one term digs it out.

{% include figure.html path="blog/overwinter-post/shot-5.jpg" caption="The aurora after a cyclone." %}

### A report for a doctor

When a doctor asks how the last month went, the app makes a plain PDF without any of the polar words: energy, mood, pain by body part, sleep, what you took, and every day in a table.

{% include figure.html path="blog/overwinter-post/shot-6.jpg" caption="The body is the station." %}

## Good to know

**Is it a medical app?**

No. It's a diary: it helps you notice patterns and bring the days to a doctor, but it doesn't diagnose anything.

**My phone has no barometer.**

Everything works, just without the pressure.

**Where does the data go?**

Nowhere. The app has no internet permission, and a PDF leaves the phone only when you send it.
